Color Scopes & Shot Matching

Reading scopes for precise color decisions and making multiple shots look consistent

Why Use Scopes?

Your eyes are unreliable when it comes to judging color and brightness. The ambient light in your room, the calibration of your monitor, and even how tired you are can all affect your perception. Scopes are objective, visual graphs that show you the actual data in your image — brightness levels, color balance, and saturation. Professional colorists rely on scopes to make accurate decisions.

To open scopes on the Color page, click the Scopes button in the bottom-right area (or go to Workspace > Video Scopes). You can display one scope at a time or view multiple scopes simultaneously.

Waveform

The Waveform monitor is the most commonly used scope. It shows the brightness distribution of your image from left to right — the left edge of the waveform corresponds to the left side of your image, and the right edge to the right side.

  • The vertical axis represents brightness: 0 at the bottom (pure black) and 1023 at the top (pure white).
  • A properly exposed image typically fills the range from just above 0 to just below 1023, with most content in the middle.
  • If the waveform is bunched up near the bottom, the image is underexposed (too dark).
  • If it's pushed against the top, the image is overexposed (too bright) and highlights are clipping — detail is lost.
Tip: A good workflow habit: the darkest thing in your shot should sit just above 0, and the brightest just below 1023 (unless there's a creative reason to clip, like a bright light source). This ensures you're using the full brightness range.

Parade

The Parade scope is similar to the waveform but separates the image into its Red, Green, and Blue channels, displayed side by side. This is incredibly useful for checking white balance:

  • If the image is perfectly white-balanced, the three channels should have roughly the same shape and height in the highlights.
  • If the Red channel is noticeably higher in the highlights than Green and Blue, the whites have a warm (orange/yellow) cast.
  • If Blue is higher, the image has a cool cast.
  • Use the Color Wheels or Temperature/Tint sliders to balance the channels.

Vectorscope

The Vectorscope is a circular display that shows the hue and saturation of your image:

  • Direction from center = the hue (color). Targets around the edge are labeled for specific colors: Red, Magenta, Blue, Cyan, Green, Yellow.
  • Distance from center = the saturation (intensity). Content near the center is desaturated (gray), while content reaching toward the edge is vivid.
  • For skin tones, look for a concentration of data along the skin tone line — a diagonal line between the Red and Yellow targets (roughly the 10 o'clock position). If skin tones drift away from this line, they may look unnatural.

Histogram

The Histogram shows the overall tonal distribution of the image. The horizontal axis goes from dark (left) to bright (right), and the height shows how many pixels fall at each brightness level.

  • A balanced image typically has data spread across most of the histogram.
  • A spike at the far right means highlights are clipping.
  • A spike at the far left means shadows are crushed (detail lost in blacks).
  • The histogram gives a quick overview but is less precise than the waveform for making corrections.

Shot Matching

When editing a scene, you often cut between multiple shots taken at different times or with different camera settings. Even if they're shot in the same location, differences in exposure, white balance, and color can make them look mismatched. Shot matching is the process of making all the clips in a scene look visually consistent.

Manual Matching

  1. Pick your hero shot — the clip that looks the best or is used the most. Grade it first.
  2. Move to the next clip. Open your scopes and compare the waveform shape to the hero shot.
  3. Adjust Lift, Gamma, and Gain to match the brightness range.
  4. Check the Parade to match white balance across RGB channels.
  5. Compare side-by-side using the Image Wipe or Split Screen modes (click the viewer mode buttons on the Color page).

Color Match Tool

DaVinci Resolve includes an automatic Color Match tool that can help speed things up:

  1. Select the clip you want to adjust.
  2. Go to Color > Color Match (or find it in the Color Wheels panel dropdown).
  3. The tool will analyze a reference clip or chart and attempt to match the current clip to it.

Auto-matching is a good starting point, but it usually needs manual refinement.

Gallery Stills

The Gallery (left side of the Color page) lets you save and reuse grades:

  1. Grade a clip to your liking.
  2. Right-click the viewer and choose Grab Still (or press Ctrl+Alt+G). This saves a snapshot of the current grade.
  3. To apply the saved grade to another clip, select the new clip, then right-click the still in the Gallery and choose Apply Grade.
Tip: You can also copy and paste grades directly. Right-click a graded clip in the Color page thumbnail strip and choose Copy. Select the target clip(s), right-click, and choose Paste. This copies all node settings.

PowerWindows & Qualifiers

Sometimes you need to adjust color in only part of the frame, not the entire image.

PowerWindows

PowerWindows are shapes (circles, rectangles, polygons, gradients) that isolate a region of the image. For example, you might brighten just a person's face or darken the sky:

  1. Add a new node for the targeted adjustment.
  2. Open the Window panel (diamond icon in the center palette).
  3. Choose a shape (circle, square, polygon, gradient, or curve).
  4. Position and size the window over the area you want to affect.
  5. Adjust the softness to create a smooth falloff edge.
  6. Now any color adjustments on this node only affect the area inside (or outside) the window.

Qualifiers

Qualifiers (also called secondary color selections) let you select a specific color range. For example, you can select the blue sky and make it deeper, or select skin tones and warm them:

  1. Add a new node.
  2. Open the Qualifier panel (eyedropper icon in the center palette).
  3. Use the eyedropper to click on the color you want to select in the viewer.
  4. Adjust the Hue, Saturation, and Luminance ranges to refine your selection.
  5. Toggle Highlight on the viewer to see what's selected (highlighted).
  6. Make adjustments — they'll only affect the qualified color range.

Key Takeaways

  • Use scopes (Waveform, Parade, Vectorscope, Histogram) for objective color decisions — don't rely on your eyes alone.
  • The Waveform shows brightness distribution; the Parade separates RGB for white balance; the Vectorscope shows hue/saturation.
  • Shot matching ensures consistency across clips — start with a hero shot and match others to it.
  • Gallery Stills let you save, compare, and apply grades across clips.
  • Use PowerWindows for area-based adjustments and Qualifiers for color-based selections.
