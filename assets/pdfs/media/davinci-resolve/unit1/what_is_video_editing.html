What Is Video Editing?

An introduction to the craft of video editing, its history, and the fundamentals every editor should know

The Craft of Video Editing

Video editing is the process of selecting, arranging, and modifying recorded footage to tell a story or convey a message. A raw recording straight from a camera is rarely ready to share — it may contain awkward pauses, shaky moments, or footage that doesn't serve the final product. The editor's job is to shape that raw material into something polished and purposeful.

Editing is often called the "invisible art" because the best edits go unnoticed by the viewer. When a cut between two shots feels natural, when music swells at just the right moment, when pacing keeps you engaged — that's skilled editing at work.

Linear vs. Non-Linear Editing

Historically, there have been two approaches to video editing:

Linear Editing

In the early days of film and video, editing was linear — you worked through footage from beginning to end. Film editors physically cut strips of celluloid and spliced them together with tape. In the analog video era, editors copied selected segments from one tape to another in sequence. If you wanted to change something in the middle, you often had to redo everything that came after it.

Non-Linear Editing (NLE)

Non-linear editing changed everything. With digital NLE software, your footage lives on a computer and you can jump to any point, rearrange clips freely, undo changes instantly, and try different approaches without destroying your original files. This is how virtually all editing is done today.

A Brief History

  • 1890s–1950s: Film was physically cut with scissors or razor blades and joined with cement or tape on a device called a flatbed editor.
  • 1960s–1980s: Analog videotape editing became common in television. Editors used linear tape-to-tape systems.
  • 1990s: Digital non-linear editors emerged. Avid Media Composer (1989) pioneered professional digital editing. Adobe Premiere launched in 1991. Apple's Final Cut Pro arrived in 1999.
  • 2000s–Present: NLEs became accessible to everyone. DaVinci Resolve, originally a high-end color grading tool, evolved into a full-featured free NLE starting around 2013.

DaVinci Resolve is a professional application that benefits from decent hardware:

Video Fundamentals

Before editing, you should understand a few key video concepts:

Resolution

Resolution describes how many pixels make up each frame of video. More pixels mean a sharper image.

NameResolutionCommon Use
720p (HD)1280 × 720Older web video, lower-end streaming
1080p (Full HD)1920 × 1080Standard for most content today
4K (UHD)3840 × 2160High-end production, YouTube/streaming premium

Frame Rate

Video is a rapid sequence of still images (frames). The number of frames shown per second determines how smooth the motion appears.

  • 24 fps — The cinematic standard. Used in films and most narrative content. Gives a slightly dreamy, filmic look.
  • 30 fps — Standard for broadcast TV, web video, and general-purpose content.
  • 60 fps — Very smooth motion. Great for sports, gaming content, and action-heavy footage. Also useful for slow-motion when slowed to 24 or 30 fps.

Aspect Ratio

Aspect ratio is the proportional relationship between width and height.

  • 16:9 — The standard widescreen ratio for YouTube, TV, and most video. Both 1080p and 4K use this ratio.
  • 9:16 — Vertical video for TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts.
  • 21:9 — Ultra-widescreen, used in cinematic films.
  • 1:1 — Square format, sometimes used on social media.
Tip: Always check the requirements of your target platform before you start editing. A YouTube video uses 16:9 at 1080p or 4K, while a TikTok uses 9:16 at 1080×1920.
