What Is Digital Audio?

Understanding how sound becomes data a computer can store and play back

Sound in the Real World

Before we can work with audio on a computer, it helps to understand what sound actually is. Sound is a vibration that travels through the air (or another medium) as a wave. When you speak, your vocal cords vibrate, pushing air molecules outward. Those vibrations reach a listener's ear — or a microphone — and are perceived as sound.

Frequency (Pitch)

Every sound wave has a frequency, which describes how many times the wave vibrates per second. Frequency is measured in Hertz (Hz).

  • A low-pitched sound (like a bass drum) might vibrate at around 60 Hz.
  • A high-pitched sound (like a whistle) might vibrate at 4,000 Hz or higher.
  • Human hearing ranges roughly from 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz.

Amplitude (Volume)

Amplitude describes the strength — or loudness — of a sound wave. Bigger vibrations mean louder sound. Amplitude in audio production is measured in decibels (dB).

  • 0 dB in digital audio is the absolute maximum level before distortion.
  • Normal speech might sit around -20 dB to -12 dB on a digital meter.
  • Anything above 0 dB causes clipping, which sounds harsh and distorted.

Analog vs. Digital Sound

In the real world, sound is analog — it's a smooth, continuous wave. A microphone converts that analog wave into an electrical signal. To store and edit sound on a computer, we need to convert that continuous signal into digital data — a series of numbers.

This conversion is done by a device called an Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC). Your computer's sound card or USB microphone has one built in. When you play the audio back, a Digital-to-Analog Converter (DAC) turns the numbers back into an electrical signal that drives your headphones or speakers.

Sampling

A digital recording works by taking rapid snapshots (called samples) of the analog wave. The number of snapshots taken per second is the sample rate.

Sample RateUse Case
44,100 Hz (44.1 kHz)CD-quality audio, music distribution
48,000 Hz (48 kHz)Video production, broadcast, streaming
96,000 Hz (96 kHz)High-resolution studio recording
Tip: For most projects in this course, 44,100 Hz is perfectly fine. If you're recording audio for video, use 48,000 Hz to match standard video sample rates.

Bit Depth

While sample rate controls how often we snapshot the wave, bit depth controls how precisely we measure each snapshot's amplitude.

  • 16-bit — CD quality. Provides 65,536 possible amplitude values per sample. Great for final distribution.
  • 24-bit — Professional recording standard. Provides over 16 million possible values, giving much more detail and a lower noise floor. Best for recording and editing.
  • 32-bit float — Used internally by Audacity. Provides virtually unlimited headroom and prevents clipping during editing.

Audio File Formats

Once audio has been recorded digitally, it needs to be saved in a file. Different formats handle this in different ways:

FormatTypeNotes
WAVUncompressedFull quality, large file size. Best for editing and archiving.
MP3Lossy compressedMuch smaller files, some quality is permanently lost. Great for sharing.
FLACLossless compressedSmaller than WAV but no quality loss. Good for archiving.
OGG VorbisLossy compressedOpen-source alternative to MP3 with similar quality.
AACLossy compressedCommon in Apple/YouTube. Generally better quality than MP3 at the same bitrate.

Bitrate (for Compressed Formats)

When saving as MP3 or another compressed format, you choose a bitrate — the amount of data used per second of audio.

  • 128 kbps — Acceptable for speech, podcasts, or casual listening.
  • 192 kbps — Good balance of quality and file size.
  • 320 kbps — Highest MP3 quality; very hard to distinguish from the original.

Mono vs. Stereo

Mono (monophonic) audio is a single channel — the same sound comes from every speaker. Stereo (stereophonic) audio has two channels — left and right — allowing sound to be positioned across the listening space.

  • Use mono for: voice recordings, podcasts, basic narration.
  • Use stereo for: music, ambient sound, any project where spatial positioning matters.
Tip: Recording a single microphone in mono keeps your file size smaller and avoids phase issues. You can always place a mono track in a stereo mix later.

Key Takeaways

  • Sound is a wave with frequency (pitch) and amplitude (volume).
  • Digital audio converts continuous analog waves into discrete numerical samples.
  • Sample rate (44.1 kHz or 48 kHz) controls how often we capture the wave; bit depth (16-bit or 24-bit) controls how precisely.
  • WAV is uncompressed and best for editing; MP3 is compressed and best for sharing.
  • Choose mono for single-voice recordings and stereo for music.
