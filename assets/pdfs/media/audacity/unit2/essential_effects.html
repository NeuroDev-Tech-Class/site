Audio Production — Audacity

Essential Audio Effects

Amplify, normalize, compress, equalize, fade, and transform your audio

Overview

Audacity comes with a large library of built-in effects. This lesson covers the effects you'll use most often in real projects. For every effect: select the audio you want to process first, then apply the effect from the Effect menu.

Amplify

Effect > Amplify increases or decreases the volume of the selected audio by a fixed amount.

  • Amplification (dB): Enter a positive number to make the audio louder or a negative number to make it quieter.
  • New Peak Amplitude: Shows what the loudest peak will be after amplification.
  • "Allow clipping" checkbox: When unchecked (default), Audacity limits the amplification so peaks don't exceed 0 dB. Check it only if you intentionally want to push past 0 dB (rarely recommended).

Amplify applies a uniform volume change — every part of the selection gets louder or quieter by the same amount.

Normalize

Effect > Normalize adjusts the audio so its peak level reaches a specific target.

  • Normalize peak amplitude to: Sets the loudest point to a target level. A common setting is -1.0 dB, which maximizes volume while leaving a tiny bit of headroom.
  • Remove DC offset: Fixes a common technical issue where the waveform isn't centered on the zero line. Always leave this checked.
  • Normalize stereo channels independently: When checked, each channel is adjusted separately. When unchecked, both channels are adjusted by the same amount (preserving the stereo balance).
Tip: Amplify and Normalize may seem similar, but there's a key difference. Amplify lets you choose how much to change the volume. Normalize lets you choose what the final peak level should be. Normalize is generally preferred as a final step because it guarantees a consistent output level.

Compressor

Effect > Compressor reduces the dynamic range of your audio — it makes quiet parts louder and loud parts quieter, resulting in a more consistent volume throughout.

The Compressor has several important settings:

SettingWhat It DoesRecommended Start
Threshold The volume level above which compression kicks in. Audio below this level is unaffected. -12 dB
Noise Floor Audio below this level is treated as noise and not boosted. -40 dB
Ratio How much the volume above the threshold is reduced. A 4:1 ratio means that for every 4 dB above the threshold, only 1 dB comes through. 4:1
Attack Time How quickly the compressor reacts when audio exceeds the threshold. Shorter attack catches transients; longer attack lets them through. 0.2 seconds
Release Time How quickly the compressor stops compressing after audio drops below the threshold. 1.0 seconds
Make-up gain When checked, automatically boosts the overall level after compression to compensate for the reduction. Checked

Compression is essential for podcast and voiceover work. It makes speech easier to listen to at a consistent volume, especially on mobile devices and in noisy environments.

Equalization / Filter Curve EQ

Effect > Filter Curve EQ (or Graphic EQ) lets you boost or cut specific frequency ranges. This is how you shape the tone of your audio.

Common EQ adjustments:

  • High-pass filter (cut below 80 Hz): Removes low-frequency rumble from handling noise, air conditioning, and vibrations. Essential for speech recordings.
  • Reduce muddiness (cut around 200–400 Hz): Cleans up "boxy" or muffled-sounding voice recordings.
  • Boost presence (boost around 2–5 kHz): Makes speech clearer and more intelligible.
  • Brighten (boost around 8–12 kHz): Adds sparkle and airiness. Use sparingly to avoid harshness.
  • Bass boost (boost around 80–200 Hz): Adds warmth and depth to music or a deep voice.
Tip: The golden rule of EQ: cut more than you boost. Cutting unwanted frequencies sounds more natural than boosting desired ones. Small adjustments (2–4 dB) go a long way.

Fade In & Fade Out

Fades smoothly ramp the volume up from silence (fade in) or down to silence (fade out). They provide clean transitions at the beginning and end of tracks or segments.

  • Fade In: Select the beginning of your audio (typically 0.5–2 seconds) and go to Effect > Fade In.
  • Fade Out: Select the end of your audio and go to Effect > Fade Out.

Cross Fade Clips

When two audio clips are adjacent on the same track, you can create a smooth transition between them:

  1. Make sure the two clips slightly overlap (or are directly adjacent).
  2. Select across the transition point.
  3. Go to Effect > Cross Fade Clips.

The first clip fades out while the second fades in, creating a seamless blend.

Reverb

Effect > Reverb adds the natural ambiance of a room to your recording. It simulates sound bouncing off walls and surfaces.

  • Room Size: Larger values simulate bigger spaces (halls, cathedrals). Smaller values simulate closets or small rooms.
  • Reverberance: How long the reverb tail lasts.
  • Damping: How quickly high frequencies fade in the reverb (higher damping = warmer, less bright reverb).
  • Wet/Dry Mix: Balance between the original signal (dry) and the reverb signal (wet). For subtle room ambiance, keep the mix mostly dry.
Warning: Reverb is easy to overuse. A small amount can make a recording feel more natural. Too much makes it sound like you're in a cave. Use sparingly, especially with speech.

Echo

Effect > Echo creates repeating copies of the sound at a set interval.

  • Delay time: The time between each repeat (in seconds).
  • Decay factor: How much quieter each repeat becomes (0 to 1). At 0.5, each echo is half the volume of the previous one.

Echo is used more for creative/artistic effects than for everyday speech editing.

Pitch, Tempo, and Speed

Audacity offers three related but distinct effects for changing how your audio sounds in time and pitch:

EffectWhat It ChangesWhat Stays the Same
Change Pitch Pitch (higher or lower) Tempo and duration stay the same
Change Tempo Speed and duration (faster or slower) Pitch stays the same
Change Speed Both pitch and tempo together Nothing — both change linked (like playing a record at the wrong speed)
  • Change Pitch is useful for tuning corrections or creative voice effects.
  • Change Tempo is useful for making a recording slightly faster or slower without the "chipmunk" or "slowed-down" effect.
  • Change Speed simulates the classic effect of speeding up or slowing down a tape.

Applying Effects: General Workflow

  1. Select the audio region you want to affect (or Ctrl+A for the whole track).
  2. Go to Effect and choose the effect.
  3. Adjust settings in the dialog.
  4. Click Preview to hear a sample of the result.
  5. Click OK to apply.
  6. If you don't like the result, press Ctrl+Z to undo and try different settings.

Key Takeaways

  • Amplify changes volume by a set amount; Normalize sets the peak to a target level.
  • Compressor evens out volume differences — essential for speech and podcasts.
  • Filter Curve EQ shapes tone by boosting or cutting frequency ranges. Cut more than you boost.
  • Fade In/Out and Cross Fade create smooth transitions.
  • Reverb adds room ambiance (use sparingly); Echo creates repeats.
  • Change Pitch, Change Tempo, and Change Speed each alter different aspects of the audio.
  • Always Preview before applying, and use Ctrl+Z freely.
