Color Correction

Fixing Exposure, White Balance, and Color in Your Images

Why Color Correct?

Photos straight from a camera often have issues: they may be too dark, too bright, have a yellow or blue color cast, or look flat and dull. Color correction is the process of adjusting these tonal and color properties so that the image looks natural and visually appealing. GIMP provides all the tools you need in the Colors menu.

Color Correction Tools

Brightness-Contrast

Colors > Brightness-Contrast is the simplest adjustment. It has two sliders:

  • Brightness — makes the entire image lighter or darker.
  • Contrast — increases or decreases the difference between light and dark areas.

While easy to use, Brightness-Contrast is a blunt tool — it affects all pixels equally, which can wash out highlights or crush shadows. For more control, use Levels or Curves.

Levels

Colors > Levels is a histogram-based adjustment tool that gives you much finer control. The dialog shows a histogram — a graph of how many pixels exist at each brightness level (dark on the left, bright on the right).

Key controls:

  • Input Levels — three sliders below the histogram:
    • Black point (left) — drag right to deepen the darkest shadows.
    • Gray point / midtone (middle) — drag left to brighten or right to darken midtones.
    • White point (right) — drag left to brighten highlights.
  • Output Levels — limits the range of the output. Rarely needed for basic corrections.
  • Channel — you can adjust the overall Value (brightness) or adjust the Red, Green, and Blue channels individually to fix color casts.
Tip: A quick way to improve most photos: open Levels and drag the black-point slider to where the histogram data begins on the left, and drag the white-point slider to where the data ends on the right. This "stretches" the tonal range and often makes images look dramatically better.

Curves

Colors > Curves is the most powerful color correction tool. It displays the same histogram as Levels, but instead of sliders, you get a diagonal line (the "curve") that you can click and drag to reshape.

  • The horizontal axis represents input brightness (original values).
  • The vertical axis represents output brightness (adjusted values).
  • Clicking a point on the curve and dragging it up brightens those tones; dragging down darkens them.
  • An S-curve (highlights pulled up, shadows pulled down) increases contrast naturally.

Like Levels, you can adjust individual Red, Green, and Blue channels to correct color casts with extreme precision.

Hue-Saturation

Colors > Hue-Saturation adjusts the color properties of the image:

  • Hue — shifts all colors around the color wheel (e.g., turning reds into oranges, blues into greens).
  • Saturation — increases or decreases color intensity. Sliding all the way left produces a grayscale image.
  • Lightness — makes the image lighter or darker (similar to brightness).

You can adjust all colors at once or select a specific color range (Red, Yellow, Green, Cyan, Blue, Magenta) to adjust individually.

Color Balance

Colors > Color Balance lets you shift the overall color of the image toward one color or its opposite. You can independently adjust:

  • Shadows — affects only the dark areas of the image.
  • Midtones — affects the middle brightness range (where most detail lives).
  • Highlights — affects only the bright areas.

For each range, three sliders let you push the color toward Cyan–Red, Magenta–Green, or Yellow–Blue. This is an intuitive way to remove unwanted color casts — for example, if a photo looks too yellow, push the midtones slider toward Blue.

Desaturate

Colors > Desaturate converts a color image to grayscale while keeping it in RGB mode. Multiple methods are available (Luminosity, Lightness, Average, etc.) — Luminosity usually produces the most natural-looking result because it weights the green channel more heavily, matching how our eyes perceive brightness.

Recommended Workflow

When color correcting a photo, follow this order for the best results:

  1. Fix exposure with Levels — set black and white points, adjust midtones.
  2. Adjust white balance — use Curves (per-channel) or Color Balance to remove color casts.
  3. Fine-tune saturation — use Hue-Saturation to boost or reduce color intensity as needed.

Reading a Histogram

The histogram tells you a lot about your image at a glance:

  • If the data is bunched up on the left, the image is underexposed (too dark).
  • If the data is bunched up on the right, the image is overexposed (too bright).
  • If there's a gap on either end, the image isn't using its full tonal range — adjust Levels to fix this.
  • A spike at the far left or right means clipping — pure black or pure white pixels with no detail.
Warning: Once highlights or shadows are clipped (pure white or pure black), the detail in those areas is gone and can't be recovered. Try to preserve detail across the full range.

Summary

  • Brightness-Contrast is simple but imprecise — use it for quick adjustments only.
  • Levels provides histogram-based control with black point, white point, and midtone sliders.
  • Curves is the most powerful tool for precise tonal and color control.
  • Hue-Saturation adjusts color intensity; Color Balance fixes color casts in shadows, midtones, and highlights.
  • Follow the workflow: Levels → White Balance → Saturation.
