Masks & Compositing

Non-Destructive Editing and Seamless Image Blending

What Are Layer Masks?

A layer mask is a grayscale image attached to a layer that controls which parts of that layer are visible and which are hidden. Think of it as an invisible stencil that sits on top of the layer:

  • White on the mask = the layer is fully visible in that area.
  • Black on the mask = the layer is fully hidden (transparent) in that area.
  • Gray on the mask = the layer is semi-transparent — the darker the gray, the more hidden.

The key advantage of layer masks is that they are non-destructive. You aren't erasing pixels — you're just hiding them. You can always paint white back on the mask to reveal hidden areas, or adjust the mask at any time without losing any data.

Adding a Layer Mask

  1. In the Layers dialog, right-click the layer you want to mask.
  2. Choose Add Layer Mask.
  3. A dialog appears asking you to initialize the mask:
    • White (full opacity) — the entire layer starts fully visible. You'll paint with black to hide areas.
    • Black (full transparency) — the entire layer starts fully hidden. You'll paint with white to reveal areas.
    • Layer's alpha channel — copies existing transparency into the mask.
    • Selection — creates a mask from the current selection.
  4. Click Add. A mask thumbnail appears next to the layer thumbnail in the Layers dialog.
Tip: Click on the mask thumbnail to edit the mask. Click on the layer thumbnail to edit the layer's pixels. A white border around the thumbnail indicates which one is active. Make sure you're editing the right one!

Painting on a Mask

With the mask selected (click its thumbnail), use any paint tool:

  • Paint with black to hide parts of the layer.
  • Paint with white to reveal parts of the layer.
  • Use a soft brush for gradual, feathered transitions between visible and hidden areas.
  • Use gray for partial transparency — great for subtle blending.

Press X to quickly swap between black and white foreground colors while painting on a mask.

The Alpha Channel

Every layer can have an alpha channel, which controls pixel-level transparency. When a layer has an alpha channel, transparent areas show as a gray checkerboard pattern. You can add an alpha channel to any layer via Layer > Transparency > Add Alpha Channel.

The difference between the alpha channel and a layer mask:

  • The alpha channel is transparency baked directly into the layer's pixels. Erasing pixels removes them permanently.
  • A layer mask is a separate, editable grayscale image that controls visibility non-destructively. The original pixels remain intact underneath.
Warning: Whenever possible, use a layer mask instead of the Eraser tool. Erasing destroys pixels permanently, while masks let you undo, refine, and adjust at any point.

The Channels Dialog

The Channels dialog (Windows > Dockable Dialogs > Channels) shows the individual color channels of your image (Red, Green, Blue) plus the Alpha channel. Each channel is a grayscale image representing the intensity of that color component. Advanced users can edit individual channels for precise color and selection work.

Compositing Workflow

Compositing is the art of combining multiple images into a single, seamless scene. Layer masks are essential for smooth compositing. Here is a step-by-step workflow:

Step 1: Place the Subject on a Separate Layer

Open both the subject image and the background image in GIMP. Copy the subject (Ctrl+C) and paste it into the background image (Ctrl+V). Go to Layer > To New Layer to convert the floating selection into its own layer. Use the Scale and Move tools to position and resize the subject.

Step 2: Add a Layer Mask

Right-click the subject layer and choose Add Layer Mask. Initialize it with White (full opacity) — the subject will be fully visible to start.

Step 3: Paint the Mask to Blend Edges

Select a soft, round brush and set the foreground color to black. Click on the mask thumbnail, then paint over the areas of the subject you want to hide — typically the hard edges around the subject, the original background that came with it, and any areas that should blend into the new background.

Use a large, soft brush along the edges for a natural fade. Zoom in and use a smaller brush for detailed areas like hair or fine edges.

Step 4: Adjust Opacity and Blend Mode

Fine-tune the composite by adjusting the subject layer's opacity and blend mode:

  • Lower opacity slightly if the subject looks too "pasted on."
  • Try blend modes like Multiply, Screen, or Soft Light for creative blending effects.
  • Match the lighting and color of the subject to the background using Curves or Color Balance.
Tip: Convincing composites depend on matching lighting direction, color temperature, and perspective between the subject and background. Pay attention to where shadows fall and which direction light is coming from.

Summary

  • Layer masks use white = visible, black = hidden, gray = semi-transparent.
  • Masks are non-destructive — you can always adjust them without losing pixels.
  • The alpha channel is built-in transparency; layer masks are separate and editable.
  • Use the compositing workflow: place subject → add mask → paint to blend → adjust opacity/blend mode.
  • Match lighting and color between layers for a seamless result.
