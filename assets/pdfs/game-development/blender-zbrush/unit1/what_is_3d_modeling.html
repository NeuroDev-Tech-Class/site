What Is 3D Modeling?

Understanding 3D modeling and why it matters

Introduction

3D modeling is the process of creating a mathematical representation of a three-dimensional object using specialized software. Think of it as digital sculpting — you're building shapes, surfaces, and structures inside a computer that can be viewed from every angle, animated, 3D printed, or dropped into a video game.

If you've ever played a video game, watched a Pixar movie, or seen an architectural rendering of a building that doesn't exist yet, you've already experienced the results of 3D modeling. It's one of the most versatile and in-demand creative skills in the modern world.

Where Is 3D Modeling Used?

3D modeling shows up in far more industries than most people realize. Here are some of the biggest ones:

Game Development

Every character, weapon, vehicle, building, and environment you see in a 3D video game was created by a 3D modeler. Games like Fortnite, Minecraft (yes, even those cubes are 3D models), and The Legend of Zelda: Tears of the Kingdom all rely on artists building assets in tools like Blender, Maya, or ZBrush.

Film & Animation

Studios like Pixar, Disney, DreamWorks, and Illumination use 3D modeling to create entire worlds and characters. Movies like Toy Story, Frozen, and Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse are built almost entirely from 3D models. Even live-action films use 3D models for visual effects — think of the dinosaurs in Jurassic World or the suits in Iron Man.

Architecture & Interior Design

Architects use 3D modeling to visualize buildings before they're constructed. Clients can take virtual tours of a house that hasn't been built yet. Interior designers use 3D scenes to experiment with furniture placement, lighting, and materials.

Product Design & Manufacturing

Before a product hits store shelves, it's often designed and tested as a 3D model. Car manufacturers, electronics companies, and furniture designers all prototype in 3D before building physical versions.

3D Printing

Every 3D-printed object starts as a 3D model. Whether you're printing a custom phone case, a replacement part for an appliance, or a miniature figurine, you need a 3D model file (usually STL or OBJ format) to send to the printer.

Virtual Reality & Augmented Reality (VR/AR)

VR experiences and AR apps need 3D content. From immersive VR games to AR filters on social media, 3D modelers create the objects and environments that users interact with.

Types of 3D Modeling

Not all 3D modeling is the same. There are several approaches, each suited for different tasks:

Polygon / Mesh Modeling

This is the most common type and what we'll focus on in this course. Objects are built from vertices (points), edges (lines connecting points), and faces (flat surfaces enclosed by edges). By manipulating these elements, you can create anything from a simple cube to a detailed spaceship. Blender excels at polygon modeling.

Digital Sculpting

Instead of moving individual vertices, sculpting lets you push, pull, smooth, and carve a digital surface — much like shaping real clay. This approach is ideal for organic shapes like characters, creatures, and natural objects. ZBrush is the industry-standard sculpting tool, and we'll get a taste of it with ZBrushCoreMini.

CAD (Computer-Aided Design)

CAD modeling uses precise measurements and mathematical shapes. It's used primarily in engineering and manufacturing where exact dimensions matter. Tools like SolidWorks, Fusion 360, and AutoCAD fall into this category. We won't be covering CAD in this course, but it's good to know it exists.

About Blender

Blender is a free, open-source 3D creation suite. That means it costs nothing to download and use, even commercially — and its source code is publicly available. Despite being free, Blender is used by professional studios around the world.

Blender started as an in-house tool at a Dutch animation studio in 1994. When the studio closed, its creator Ton Roosendaal launched a crowdfunding campaign to release the software as open-source. Since then, Blender has grown into one of the most powerful 3D tools available, rivaling software that costs thousands of dollars per year.

Blender can do far more than just modeling — it includes tools for animation, rigging, rendering, video editing, physics simulation, and even 2D drawing. In this course, we'll focus on its modeling and material features.

Tip: You can download Blender for free at blender.org. It runs on Windows, macOS, and Linux.

About ZBrushCoreMini

ZBrushCoreMini is a free, simplified version of ZBrush — the industry-leading digital sculpting software. It's designed specifically as an introduction to sculpting for beginners. While the full version of ZBrush is a paid professional tool, ZBrushCoreMini gives you access to core sculpting brushes so you can experience what digital sculpting feels like.

We'll use ZBrushCoreMini later in this course to explore sculpting as a complement to the polygon modeling we do in Blender.
