3D Printing Basics

How FDM Printing Works — From Filament to Finished Object

What Is 3D Printing?

3D printing (also called additive manufacturing) is the process of creating a physical object from a digital 3D model. Unlike traditional manufacturing, which cuts or carves material away (subtractive), 3D printing adds material layer by layer to build up the final shape. It's like building with microscopic layers of hot glue, each one stacking on top of the last.

How FDM Printing Works

The type of 3D printer we'll be using is called an FDM printer — Fused Deposition Modeling. Here's the basic process:

  1. A spool of filament (a long, thin strand of plastic) feeds into the printer.
  2. The filament is pushed into the extruder (also called the cold end), which grips and feeds the filament forward.
  3. The filament enters the hot end (the nozzle), which heats it to around 200°C (392°F) — hot enough to melt the plastic.
  4. The melted plastic is deposited through the tiny nozzle opening onto the build platform (the flat surface at the bottom).
  5. The nozzle moves in precise patterns, laying down one thin layer of plastic at a time.
  6. Each new layer bonds to the one below it as the plastic cools.
  7. The build platform moves down (or the nozzle moves up) by a tiny amount, and the next layer begins.
  8. This process repeats — sometimes for hours — until the entire object is complete.

Printer Parts You Should Know

  • Build Platform (Bed): The flat surface where your print is created. Some beds are heated to help the first layer stick. The bed should be clean and level for good prints.
  • Extruder / Cold End: The motor-driven mechanism that grabs the filament and pushes it toward the hot end. Think of it as the "feeding" mechanism.
  • Nozzle / Hot End: The heated tip where filament melts and is deposited. Standard nozzle diameter is 0.4mm. ⚠️ VERY HOT — do NOT touch the nozzle during or shortly after printing! It reaches temperatures of 200°C or higher.
  • Filament Spool: A roll of plastic filament, usually mounted on a holder on top of or beside the printer. Filament comes in many colors and materials.
  • Frame and Rails: The metal structure and guide rails that allow the nozzle to move precisely in the X, Y, and Z directions.

Common Material: PLA

PLA (Polylactic Acid) is the most popular filament for beginners, and it's what we'll primarily use in this class. Here's why:

  • Easy to print: PLA prints at lower temperatures and doesn't warp as much as other materials.
  • Biodegradable: PLA is made from renewable resources (like cornstarch) and is more environmentally friendly than petroleum-based plastics.
  • Low odor: Unlike some other filaments, PLA produces minimal fumes during printing.
  • Comes in many colors: You can get PLA in nearly any color, plus specialty finishes like metallic, glow-in-the-dark, and wood-fill.
  • Rigid and detailed: PLA holds fine detail well, making it great for figurines, decorative objects, and prototypes.

Limitations: PLA is not heat-resistant (it softens around 60°C / 140°F) and is relatively brittle compared to materials like PETG or ABS.

Layer Height and Quality

Layer height is one of the most important settings in 3D printing. It controls how thick each individual layer of plastic is.

  • Thinner layers (0.1 - 0.15mm): Produce smoother surfaces with less visible layer lines. The print looks more detailed and refined. However, thinner layers mean more layers total, so printing takes significantly longer.
  • Thicker layers (0.2 - 0.3mm): Print much faster but have more visible "staircase" lines on curved or angled surfaces. Good for prototypes or objects where appearance isn't critical.

Think of it like resolution on a screen: more pixels (thinner layers) = sharper image, but more data to process.

Overhangs and the 45-Degree Rule

Since FDM printing builds layer by layer from the bottom up, each layer needs something underneath it to rest on. When part of a model extends outward with nothing below it, that's called an overhang.

The general rule of thumb is the 45-degree rule: if a surface angles outward at less than 45 degrees from vertical, it can usually print fine — each new layer extends just slightly beyond the one below it, and the plastic is stiff enough to hold. But if the angle exceeds 45 degrees (approaching horizontal), the plastic has nothing to grip onto and will sag, droop, or fall — creating a messy print.

Supports

When your model has steep overhangs or floating sections, you need supports — temporary structures that the printer builds underneath overhanging areas to hold them up during printing. You remove them after the print finishes.

Types of Supports

  • Linear (Grid/Lines): Traditional support style. Creates a dense grid of thin walls under overhangs. Reliable and strong, but can be harder to remove and may leave marks on the surface.
  • Tree Supports: Branch-like structures that grow upward and reach out to support overhangs. They use less material, are often easier to remove, and leave fewer marks. Many modern slicers support this option.

Bed Adhesion: Raft vs. Brim

Getting the first layer to stick to the build platform is crucial. If it doesn't stick, the entire print can shift or fail. Two common helpers:

  • Brim: A thin, flat border of extra material printed around the base of your object. It increases the contact area with the bed, helping the object stick. After printing, you peel the brim off. Best for most situations.
  • Raft: A thick platform of material printed underneath your entire object. The object is then printed on top of the raft. Rafts provide excellent adhesion but use more material and can affect the bottom surface quality. Use a raft when your object has a very small footprint or you're having persistent sticking problems.

Safety Tips

  • 🔥 Never touch the nozzle during or shortly after printing. It's hot enough to cause serious burns.
  • Don't reach into the printer while it's moving. The motors are strong and the moving parts can pinch.
  • Ventilation: While PLA is low-odor, it's still a good idea to use the printer in a well-ventilated area.
  • Use tools (scraper, tweezers) to remove prints and supports from the bed — don't use your fingers near hot surfaces.
  • Supervise prints: Don't start a long print and leave it completely unattended. Check on it periodically.
  • Keep filament dry: Moisture can cause printing problems. Store unused filament in a sealed bag with desiccant packets.

Key Takeaways

  • FDM printers build objects layer by layer from melted plastic filament.
  • PLA is the best filament for beginners — easy, safe, and eco-friendly.
  • Layer height trades quality for speed — thinner is smoother but slower.
  • Keep the 45-degree rule in mind when designing your models.
  • Use supports for overhangs and brims/rafts for bed adhesion.
  • Safety first — the nozzle is extremely hot, and moving parts can pinch.
