Slicing & Print Settings

Turning a 3D Model into Printer-Ready Instructions

What Is Slicing?

A 3D printer can't read a Blender file directly. It needs a set of precise, step-by-step instructions telling the nozzle exactly where to move, when to extrude plastic, and how fast to go. This instruction set is called G-code, and the process of converting a 3D model into G-code is called slicing.

The name comes from the fact that the software literally "slices" your 3D model into hundreds or thousands of thin horizontal layers — each layer becomes one pass of the printer nozzle. A slicer is the software that does this conversion.

Common Slicer Software

Several free slicer programs are widely used. They all do the same basic job but differ in interface and features:

  • UltiMaker Cura: One of the most popular free slicers. Great for beginners with sensible defaults and a clean interface. Supports a huge range of printers.
  • PrusaSlicer: Developed by Prusa Research. Excellent slicer with advanced features like paint-on supports and adaptive layer height. Also free and open-source.
  • Bambu Studio: Designed for Bambu Lab printers. Based on PrusaSlicer with additional features specific to Bambu hardware. If you're using a Bambu printer, this is the go-to choice.

The workflow is similar across all slicers, so the skills you learn with one transfer easily to others.

Loading Your Model

To bring your 3D model into the slicer, you need to export it from Blender in a format the slicer can read. The two most common formats are:

  • STL (Stereolithography): The most widely used 3D printing format. It stores only the surface geometry — no color, no textures, just the shape. This is the standard for most printing.
  • OBJ (Wavefront Object): Also common and supported by most slicers. Can include color information, though most FDM printers don't use it.

In the slicer, you typically just drag and drop the file into the window, or use File → Open / Import. The model will appear on a virtual representation of your printer's build plate.

Key Print Settings

Every slicer lets you adjust settings that control print quality, speed, and strength. Here are the most important ones:

Layer Height

How thick each layer is. 0.2mm is a good all-around default. Use 0.12mm for fine detail, or 0.28mm for fast drafts. This is the single biggest factor in print quality and time.

Infill Percentage

3D prints are usually not solid inside — they have an internal pattern called infill. The percentage controls how dense that pattern is:

  • 10-15%: Light and fast. Good for decorative objects that won't bear weight.
  • 20-30%: A solid middle ground for most prints.
  • 50%+: Strong and heavy. Use for functional parts that need to handle stress.
  • 100%: Completely solid. Uses the most material and takes the longest, but maximum strength.

Print Speed

How fast the nozzle moves while printing. Slower speeds generally produce cleaner results. Most slicers default to around 50-60 mm/s, which is a good starting point. You can go faster (80-100+ mm/s) for quick drafts, or slower (30-40 mm/s) for fine detail work.

Temperature

Nozzle temperature controls how hot the filament gets. For PLA, this is typically 190-210°C. Bed temperature (if your printer has a heated bed) is usually 50-60°C for PLA. The filament manufacturer's recommendation (printed on the spool or box) is the best starting point.

Supports

Toggle supports on or off depending on your model. Most slicers can automatically detect overhangs and generate supports where needed. You can usually choose between regular (grid/linear) and tree supports. Some slicers also let you paint support areas manually for more control.

Raft / Brim

Choose a bed adhesion method (or none). A brim is the most common choice — it adds a thin border around the base. A raft builds a full platform underneath for tricky models. For most prints on a well-leveled bed, a brim or even no adhesion helper is sufficient.

Orienting Your Model

How you position your model on the virtual build plate matters a lot:

  • Flat side down: Place the largest flat surface on the build plate for stability and good adhesion.
  • Minimize overhangs: Rotate the model so fewer areas stick out horizontally. This reduces the need for supports.
  • Surface quality: The side facing up will generally have the smoothest finish. The side touching the bed (or raft) may have a slightly different texture.
  • Strength: Printed layers have the weakest bond between them. Orient the model so the layer lines don't run perpendicular to the main stress direction.

Scaling

In the slicer, you can scale your model up or down. This is useful if your Blender model is the wrong size for the real world. Most slicers show dimensions in millimeters and let you scale by percentage or by entering exact dimensions. Always double-check the size before printing — it's easy to accidentally print something too small or too large.

Previewing Layers

After you click "Slice" (or "Prepare"), the slicer generates all the layers and usually shows a layer preview. You can scrub through the layers one by one with a slider to see exactly what the printer will do at each height. This is your last chance to catch problems:

  • Are supports placed where they need to be?
  • Is the infill pattern reasonable?
  • Does the first layer have good coverage on the build plate?
  • Are there any suspiciously thin walls or missing sections?

Sending to the Printer

Once you're happy with the preview, you save or export the G-code. Depending on your printer, you can:

  • Save to SD card / USB drive: Transfer the G-code file to a memory card, insert it into the printer, and start the print from the printer's menu.
  • Send over Wi-Fi / network: Many modern printers (like Bambu Lab models) connect to your network. You can send the print job directly from the slicer.
  • USB cable: Some printers can receive G-code directly via USB connection.

Print Time Estimation

After slicing, the software will display an estimated print time and material usage. Small objects might take 30 minutes to an hour. Larger or more detailed prints can take several hours — or even a full day. Use this estimate to plan your printing. The slicer also shows how much filament (in grams or meters) the print will use.

Key Takeaways

  • A slicer converts your 3D model (STL/OBJ) into G-code instructions for the printer.
  • Layer height is the biggest quality/speed tradeoff.
  • Infill controls how solid (and strong) the inside of your print is.
  • Orient your model to minimize supports and maximize quality.
  • Always preview layers before sending to the printer.
  • Check the estimated time so you know what to expect.
