Intro to Unreal Engine

What Is a Game Engine?

Understanding the software that powers modern games — and why Unreal Engine 5 is an industry leader

The Big Picture

Imagine trying to build a house completely from scratch — mining your own iron for nails, felling trees for lumber, mixing sand into glass for the windows. You could do it, but it would take a lifetime. A game engine is like a fully stocked workshop with power tools, blueprints, and pre-cut materials. It handles the heavy, repetitive technical work so that you can focus on what makes your game unique: the ideas, the art, and the fun.

What Does a Game Engine Actually Do?

At its core, a game engine is a collection of interconnected systems that work together every single frame — often 60 times per second or more. Here are the major systems you'll find in any modern engine:

System What It Does Example
Rendering Draws 3D models, textures, and lighting to the screen Displaying a detailed castle with realistic shadows
Physics Simulates gravity, collisions, and object interactions A ball rolling down a ramp and bouncing off a wall
Input Reads player actions from keyboard, mouse, or controller Pressing W to move forward
Audio Plays sounds and music with spatial positioning Footsteps getting louder as a character walks closer
Scripting / Logic Lets creators define rules, behaviors, and game states "When the player picks up a key, unlock the door"
Animation Moves characters and objects smoothly over time A character's walk cycle blending into a run
Networking Synchronizes game state across multiple players online An online battle royale with 100 players

Without an engine, a developer would have to write all of these systems from the ground up for every single project. Engines let entire teams — or solo creators — skip that work and jump straight into building their game.

A Brief History of Game Engines

The concept of a reusable game engine emerged in the early 1990s:

  • id Tech (1993): John Carmack's engine powered Doom and Quake. id Software licensed the engine to other studios, popularizing the idea that one engine could power many different games.
  • Unreal Engine (1998): Epic Games released the original Unreal and began licensing the Unreal Engine. It has since evolved through five major versions and powers hundreds of AAA titles.
  • Unity (2005): Unity democratized game development by offering a free tier and targeting a wide range of platforms. It became especially popular for mobile and indie games.
  • Godot (2014): A free, open-source engine that has grown rapidly, favored by hobbyists and small teams.

Today, Unreal Engine and Unity dominate the industry, but Unreal has a particular stronghold in high-fidelity, large-scale projects.

Why Unreal Engine 5?

We chose Unreal Engine 5 (UE5) for this course for several compelling reasons:

1. It's Free for Education and Learning

Epic Games provides Unreal Engine completely free to download and use. You only pay royalties if you ship a commercial product that earns over $1 million in revenue — so for students and hobbyists, it's 100% free.

2. Blueprint Visual Scripting

UE5 includes Blueprints, a powerful visual scripting system that lets you create game logic by connecting nodes on a graph instead of writing code line by line. This makes Unreal approachable for beginners while still being powerful enough for professional studios.

3. Industry Standard

Unreal Engine powers some of the most successful games ever made, including Fortnite, Rocket League, the Final Fantasy VII Remake series, and many more. Learning UE5 means learning the same tool that professional developers use every day.

4. Beyond Games

Unreal Engine isn't just for games anymore. It's widely used in:

  • Film & TV — Virtual production stages (like those used for The Mandalorian)
  • Architecture — Interactive walkthroughs of buildings before they're built
  • Automotive — Virtual car configurators and showrooms
  • Simulation & Training — Military, medical, and flight simulators

5. Next-Gen Technology

UE5 introduced two groundbreaking systems:

  • Nanite — Virtualized geometry that lets you import film-quality 3D assets with millions of polygons, and the engine intelligently decides how much detail to render based on distance and screen size. No more painstakingly creating low-poly versions of your models.
  • Lumen — A fully dynamic global illumination system. Light bounces realistically off surfaces in real time, without the need to pre-bake lighting. Move a wall, and the lighting updates instantly.
