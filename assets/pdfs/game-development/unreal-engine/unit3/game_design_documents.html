Game Design Documents

Plan your game before you build it — a guide to writing a GDD

What Is a GDD?

A Game Design Document (GDD) is a written plan that describes everything about your game: what kind of game it is, how it plays, what the player does, and what the world looks and feels like. It's the blueprint for your game — before you open Unreal Engine, you should know what you're building.

Professional studios write GDDs that can be dozens or even hundreds of pages long. For our projects, we'll create shorter GDDs — usually 1–2 pages — but the habit of planning first is the same skill that professionals use every day.

Why You Need One (Even for Small Projects)

It's tempting to skip the planning step and jump straight into building. But without a plan, projects tend to go in circles:

  • Scope creep — you keep adding ideas until the project is too big to finish.
  • Lost direction — halfway through, you forget what the game was supposed to be about.
  • Wasted time — you build features that don't fit together or that you later throw away.
  • Hard to collaborate — if a teammate asks "what's the game about?", you have no clear answer.

A GDD solves all of these. It's a quick reference that keeps you focused, prevents feature bloat, and makes sure everyone on the team (even if the team is just you) agrees on the vision.

Think of it like a recipe: You could cook without one, but you'd probably forget an ingredient, add too much of something, or end up with something completely different from what you wanted. A GDD keeps your game on track.

Key Sections of a GDD

Your GDD doesn't need to be fancy — a simple document with the following sections is enough to guide a small project.

1. Title

The name of your game. It can be a working title that you change later — just give your project an identity from day one.

2. Author(s)

Who is making this game? List every team member and their role (designer, builder, artist, etc.).

3. Genre

What category does your game fall into? Examples: Platformer, RPG, Puzzle, Adventure, Horror, Racing, Shooter, Sandbox. You can combine genres: "Puzzle-Platformer" or "Action-Adventure."

4. Theme & Setting

Where and when does the game take place? What's the vibe? Examples:

  • "A medieval fantasy castle overrun with monsters"
  • "A futuristic space station with low gravity"
  • "A colorful candy world where everything is edible"

5. Target Audience

Who is this game for? Kids? Teens? Hardcore gamers? Casual players? This affects every design decision — a game for young kids will have simpler controls and brighter colors than one for experienced players.

6. Core Mechanics

List 3–5 main gameplay mechanics — the actions the player will do most often. These are the pillars of your game. Examples:

  • Jumping between platforms
  • Collecting coins and unlocking doors
  • Avoiding enemies
  • Exploring to find hidden keys
  • Using jump pads to reach new areas
Keep it to 3–5 mechanics max. A focused game with 3 polished mechanics is better than a scattered game with 10 half-done mechanics. If you can't describe the core gameplay in a short list, your game might be trying to do too much.

7. Reference Images / Mood Board

Collect 3–5 images that capture the look and feel you're going for. These can be screenshots from other games, concept art, photos, or even color palettes. Paste them into your document or pin them on a shared board.

Mood boards help you make consistent art and design decisions. When you're unsure whether to make a room dark or bright, gritty or colorful — look at your mood board.

8. Scope & Timeline

How much time do you have? What can you realistically finish? Be honest here — it's better to plan a small, complete game than an ambitious game you'll never finish.

  • List the features you must have (core mechanics, at least one level).
  • List features that would be nice to have (extra levels, polish, animations).
  • Set deadlines: rough layout by Week 1, gameplay working by Week 2, polish by Week 3, etc.

9. The "Hollywood Pitch"

Summarize your entire game in 1–2 sentences — as if you're pitching it to a movie studio. This forces you to crystallize what makes your game special. Examples:

  • "A platformer where you collect enchanted coins to unlock a castle guarded by a dragon — but you only have 3 minutes."
  • "A puzzle game set inside a clockwork machine where every gear you turn changes the level layout."
  • "An escape room in a haunted mansion where the rooms shift every time you die."

If you can't summarize your game in 1–2 sentences, you probably don't have a clear enough vision yet — and that's okay. Keep refining until you can.

Summary

  • A Game Design Document (GDD) is a written plan for your game — write it before you start building.
  • Key sections: Title, Author, Genre, Theme/Setting, Target Audience, Core Mechanics, References, Scope/Timeline, and a Hollywood Pitch.
  • Keep your mechanics list to 3–5 items to stay focused.
  • The Hollywood Pitch distills your game into 1–2 sentences — if you can't do this, keep refining your vision.
  • Your GDD is a living document — update it as the project evolves.
