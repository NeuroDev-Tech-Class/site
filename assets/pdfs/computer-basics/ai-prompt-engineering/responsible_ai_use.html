Responsible AI Use

Misinformation, privacy, and using AI honestly in school.

AI tools are genuinely useful, but using them well means understanding where they can go wrong — both in the content they produce and in how you choose to use them. This reading covers three things every student should keep in mind.

Misinformation & Deepfakes

Because generative AI can produce convincing text, images, audio, and video, it can also produce convincing fakes. A “deepfake” is media — often a photo or video of a real person — that an AI generated or altered to show something that never actually happened.

This matters in two directions:

  • What you consume. Don’t assume a photo, video, or quote is real just because it looks polished. Check who published it and whether reliable sources report the same thing.
  • What you create. Making a fake image or video of a real person — a classmate, a teacher, a public figure — can be hurtful, and in many cases illegal. Never create or share media designed to deceive people or impersonate someone.

Remember the lesson from the hands-on exercises: AI will sometimes hallucinate, stating false information with total confidence. The fix is the same for AI output as it is for anything online — fact-check before you trust or share.

Privacy: Think Before You Paste

When you type something into an AI tool, that information leaves your device and is sent to a company’s servers. Depending on the tool and its settings, your conversations may be stored and even used to help improve future models. So before you paste, ask: would I be comfortable if this weren’t private?

Don’t share sensitive information with AI tools: passwords, full credit-card or bank details, government ID numbers, your home address, or other people’s personal information. Treat the chat box like a postcard, not a diary.

Most tools offer privacy controls — options to turn off training on your chats, or to delete your history. It’s worth finding those settings in whichever tool you use.

Using AI Honestly in School

AI can be one of the best study partners you’ve ever had — or a fast way to cheat yourself out of actually learning. The difference is in how you use it.

Great uses

  • Explaining a confusing concept in simpler terms
  • Quizzing you to prepare for a test
  • Giving feedback on a draft you wrote
  • Brainstorming ideas to get unstuck

Problem uses

  • Submitting AI-written work as your own
  • Having it do an assignment meant to build your skills
  • Trusting its answers without checking them
  • Using it where your teacher has said not to

Two simple rules go a long way: follow your teacher’s policy (when in doubt, ask), and make sure you could explain and defend any work you turn in. If AI did the thinking and you couldn’t reproduce it, you didn’t learn it — and that’s the part that counts.
